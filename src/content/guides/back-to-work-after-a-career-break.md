## On your resume

Do not hide the gap. A recruiter will notice it. An unexplained gap raises more questions than an explained one.

Add it as a line in your work history, in the right place on the timeline.

> Career break, March 2023 to July 2026. Caring for a parent.

> Career break, June 2024 to August 2026. Raising my daughter. Completed a certificate in data analysis.

> Career break, January 2025 to September 2026. Health. Fully recovered and ready to work.

One line is enough. You do not owe anyone medical or family details.

LinkedIn also lets you add a career break to the Experience section, so your profile and your resume tell the same story.

## In the interview

Say it once, briefly and without apology, then move to what you can do now.

> I took two years out to care for my father. He is settled now. I am ready to come back full time. During the break I kept up with the field through a course on product analytics. I am keen to put it to work.

> I left work when my son was born and chose to stay home for three years. He started school this year. I have spent the last few months refreshing my skills and I am looking for a role where I can pick up from where I left off.

If they ask a follow-up, answer it honestly and return to the role.

## Show your skills are current

The worry behind most questions about a break is whether you are out of date. Answer it before they ask.

- **A recent course or certificate** in your field.
- **A small project** you can show, even an unpaid one.
- **Freelance or part-time work** during or after the break.
- **Volunteering** that used your professional skills.

## Routes back in

- **Returnship programmes.** Several large companies in India run them for people returning after a break. Search the company name with "returnship" or "return to work".
- **Your former employer.** People who already know your work are the easiest door to reopen.
- **Former colleagues,** now at other companies, who can refer you.
- **Contract or project roles** that turn into permanent ones.

## Who to ask for references

Your best referees are probably from before the break. That is fine. What they saw of your work still counts.

- **A former manager,** even one who has since left that company. Our guide on [who to ask when your manager has left](/guides/reference-when-your-manager-has-left/) covers this.
- **A course instructor or project mentor** from the break.
- **A freelance client** or the coordinator of the place you volunteered.
