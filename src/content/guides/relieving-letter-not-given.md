## Why it gets held up

Most delays have a dull cause.

- Your full and final settlement is not closed yet.
- A laptop, an ID card or an access card has not been returned.
- The notice period was not served in full and the buyout is unpaid.
- The handover was not signed off.
- The company is small and nobody owns the task.

A few have a harder cause: a dispute, a bond or an exit that went badly.

## Step 1: clear your side

Go through this list before you chase anyone.

- Notice period served or the buyout paid
- Handover done and confirmed by email
- Company property returned, with an acknowledgement
- No-dues or exit form completed
- Your resignation and its acceptance saved as emails

If something is pending, close it first. It removes the only fair reason to refuse.

## Step 2: ask in writing

Phone calls leave no record. Use email, copy your manager and name a date.

### The first request

> Subject: Request for relieving letter, employee ID 20418
>
> Hi,
>
> My last working day was 30 September 2026. I have served my notice, handed over my work to Sneha and returned my laptop and ID card.
>
> Please share my relieving letter and experience letter by 10 October, as my new employer needs them at joining. Do tell me if anything is pending from my side.
>
> Thank you,
> Aarav Mehta

### The follow-up, a week later

> Hi, I am following up on my email of 3 October about my relieving letter. I have not had a reply. Could you tell me the date by which I will receive it? If something is holding it up, please tell me what. My joining formalities depend on it.

### The escalation

Send this to the head of HR or a director if two emails have gone unanswered.

> Subject: Relieving letter pending since 30 September
>
> Dear Ms. Rao,
>
> I left the company on 30 September 2026 after serving my full notice. I have written to HR on 3 and 10 October for my relieving letter and have had no reply. Both emails are below.
>
> All my exit formalities are complete. I request your help in getting the letter issued this week.
>
> Regards,
> Aarav Mehta

## Step 3: tell your new employer

Do this early. Hiring teams see this problem often. It is far better heard from you than found during verification.

> Hi Ritika, I want to flag something before my joining date. My previous employer has not yet issued my relieving letter, though my exit formalities are complete. I have asked in writing twice and can share those emails. In the meantime I can give you my accepted resignation, my last three payslips and my EPFO service history. Please tell me what else would help.

### What a new employer may accept instead

- Your resignation email and its acceptance
- Your last three payslips
- Form 16 for the year
- Bank statements showing salary credits
- Your appointment letter
- Your service history from the EPFO member portal, which lists each employer with the dates you joined and left. The portal also lets you record your own date of exit if your employer has not.
- A signed undertaking that you will submit the letter when you get it

Each employer decides this for itself. Ask their HR which combination they will take.

## Step 4: if they still refuse

No single central law requires a document called a relieving letter. Some state rules for shops and establishments do require an employer to give a service certificate when someone leaves. So do the standing orders that apply to larger industrial establishments. The rules vary by state and by the kind of workplace.

In practice people take these routes, usually in this order.

1. **A lawyer's notice.** It shows you are serious and creates a record. Many employers respond at this stage.
2. **A complaint to the labour department** in your state, with your emails attached.
3. **A civil court**, as a last resort.

This is general guidance and not legal advice. Speak to a labour lawyer about your own case before you act.

## What not to do

- **Do not forge or edit a letter.** Background verification checks with the employer. An offer can be withdrawn and it can follow you for years.
- **Do not go quiet.** Keep following up in writing, politely, with dates.
- **Do not vent in public.** It rarely speeds things up and your next employer may see it.

## If you did not serve your notice

Then the employer has a reason. The way out is to settle it. Ask HR what the buyout amount is, pay it and ask for the letter in the same email. If you cannot agree, ask for a letter that states the facts: your dates, your designation and that the notice was not served in full. Many employers will accept that with an explanation.

## Where GoodWord fits and where it does not

A GoodWord report is not a relieving letter and cannot stand in for one. It does not confirm your employment dates. You still need the document from your employer or the other proof listed above.

What it can do is show a new employer what you were like to work with while the paperwork is stuck. A manager or a colleague can still speak for your work, even if they have left the company too.
