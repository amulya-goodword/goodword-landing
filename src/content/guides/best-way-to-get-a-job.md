## Why a good word beats a good resume

Hiring is a decision about risk. A recruiter has a few minutes and a hundred resumes that all say "results-driven". A resume is you describing yourself, so it cannot settle the question.

What settles it is other people. When someone the recruiter has reason to believe says "I worked with her and I would do it again", the risk drops. Psychologists call this **social proof**. We trust what others say about a person more than what that person says about themselves.

The research points the same way.

- A field experiment in South Africa, published in the [American Economic Journal: Applied Economics in 2020](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/role-reference-letters-and-skill-accreditation-south-african-labour-market), sent the same job applications with and without a reference letter from a former employer. Replies from employers rose from 4.2% to 6.7%, which is 60% more. The applicants were unemployed people aged 18 to 34.
- An [eye-tracking study by the job site Ladders in 2018](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/) found that recruiters spent an average of 7.4 seconds on their first look at a resume.
- A [2024 survey by Canva and the research firm Sago](https://tech.co/news/half-job-seekers-using-ai) asked 5,000 job seekers in eight countries about AI. In India, 74% had used generative AI on their resume, the highest of any country surveyed. When most resumes are polished by the same tools, they tell a recruiter less.
- A study of nine large firms, published in the [Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2015](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-value-of-hiring-through-employee-referrals/), found that referred applicants were more likely to be hired than other applicants with similar skills.
- The hiring software company Lever looked at about 1,000 employers in 2016. It found that 1 in 16 referred candidates was hired, against 1 in 152 who applied through a careers page or a job posting. [SHRM reported the figures](https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/lever-study-shows-1-100-candidates-hired).

Most of these come from outside India and some are several years old, so treat the exact numbers with care. None of them measured GoodWord. The direction is what matters.

## Five routes to a job, ranked by the proof they carry

### 1. A referral from someone inside

The strongest route. An employee puts their own name next to yours.

Start with people who have seen your work: former colleagues, managers and clients who now work somewhere you would like to. Make it easy for them. Send the job link, your resume and two lines on why you fit.

> Hi Sneha, I saw that your team is hiring a product analyst. Here is the link. We worked together on the pricing project for a year, so you know how I work. Would you be comfortable referring me? My resume is attached. Completely fine if you would rather not.

If you do not know anyone there, do not open with a request for a referral. Ask for ten minutes of advice.

> Hi Rohit, I am a data analyst with four years in retail. I am interested in the analytics team at your company and noticed you joined it last year. Could I ask you two or three questions about the work? Ten minutes on a call or a few lines here would help a lot.

Some people will offer to refer you after that conversation. Many will not. Thank them either way.

### 2. A direct message to the person hiring, with proof attached

When nobody inside can vouch for you, bring your own vouchers. Write to the recruiter or the hiring manager and attach evidence from people who have worked with you.

> Hi Ritika, I have applied for the Senior Analyst role on your careers page. I have attached my resume and what three people I have worked with say about my work, including my last manager. I would welcome a short call if the profile fits.

This works because it answers the recruiter's real question before they ask it.

### 3. Being found

Recruiters search LinkedIn and Naukri every day. Many filter for profiles that were active recently.

- Update your profile every few weeks, even slightly.
- Write a headline that names the role you want.
- Ask two or three people for a LinkedIn recommendation. It is social proof that sits where recruiters already look.

### 4. Job portals and careers pages

Keep applying. Many companies only hire through their own process. But a portal application alone has the lowest hit rate of the five.

Raise it by pairing every application with one message to a person at the company. Apply in the first few days of a posting. Use a plain resume on portals, because many of them read it with software.

### 5. Campus hiring, walk-ins and consultancies

For freshers and for high-volume roles these are often the main door. References matter little here. Tests and interviews decide.

The same is true of government jobs and any role filled through an exam. No reference changes your rank. Prepare for the exam.

## Build your proof before you need it

- **Ask for a reference when a project or a job ends.** The work is fresh and people are easy to reach. Our guide on [how to ask](/guides/how-to-ask-for-a-reference/) has the words.
- **Keep work you can show.** A portfolio, a case study or a number you moved.
- **Stay in touch with old colleagues.** They are your future referrals. A message twice a year is enough.

## What social proof cannot do

It gets your resume read. It does not get you the job. You still need the skills and you still need to do well in the interview.

Do not fake it. Invented references fall apart at the first phone call. [Background verification](/guides/reference-check-vs-background-verification/) catches false claims about where you worked.

Never pay for a referral or a job. Real employers and real referrers do not charge.

## A plan for one week

1. Send five targeted applications. Pair each with a message to one person at that company.
2. Ask two people for a referral.
3. Reconnect with one former colleague, with no request.
4. Update your LinkedIn and Naukri profiles.
