## Your manager is still your manager

A reference is one person's account of working with you. It belongs to the person, not to the company. When your manager changes jobs, their view of your work goes with them.

Recruiters in India deal with this all the time. People change jobs every two or three years, so a referee who now works somewhere else is normal. Give the recruiter their current designation and add one line on how you worked together.

### How to find them

- **LinkedIn.** Most managers keep their profile current. Send a message there first.
- **A mutual colleague.** Someone from the old team usually has their number.
- **Their personal email.** If you have it, use it. Their old work address is dead.

### A message to reconnect

> Hi Anand, it has been a while. I hope the new role is treating you well. I am applying for senior analyst positions. You managed me for two years at the old place, so you know my work better than anyone I could name. Would you be willing to be a reference? It would mean one short call with a recruiter. No pressure at all if the timing is wrong.

## If you cannot reach them

Pick the person who saw the most of your actual work. In rough order:

1. **A skip-level manager.** Your manager's manager, if they knew your work and not only your name.
2. **Another manager you worked with.** A project lead or someone you reported to on a dotted line.
3. **A senior peer.** Someone who worked on the same project and can describe what you did.
4. **Someone who reported to you.** A strong choice if the role you want involves leading people.
5. **A client or a vendor.** Someone outside the company who depended on you.

HR is not on this list. HR can confirm your dates and designation, which is employment verification. It is a different thing from a reference. Our guide on [reference checks and background verification](/guides/reference-check-vs-background-verification/) explains the difference.

## Four common situations

### The company has shut down

Companies close. The people you worked with are still around. Ask your former manager or a colleague wherever they work now, the same way as above.

Proving that you worked there is a separate question. Your appointment letter, payslips, Form 16 and EPFO service history all show it. [This guide](/guides/reference-letter-vs-experience-letter-vs-relieving-letter/) covers what to do when an employer cannot give you a letter.

### You are still employed and cannot tell your manager

This is the most common case of all. Say it plainly to the recruiter. They hear it every week.

Offer people who already know you are looking or who no longer work with you: a previous manager, a peer who has left or a client you trust. Many employers are happy to speak to your current employer only after you have accepted an offer.

### You left on bad terms

Do not name someone who will be lukewarm about you. A flat reference hurts more than a missing one.

Name someone else who saw the same work. If the recruiter asks why your manager is not on the list, answer in one honest sentence and do not blame anyone. For example: "We disagreed about the direction of the team and I would rather you heard from people who worked with me day to day."

### It was a long time ago

An older reference is still worth having, if that person saw important work. Remind them of the project and the year when you ask. Try to pair them with someone more recent.

## What to tell the recruiter

> Hi Ritika, here are three people who can speak about my work. Anand Rao was my manager at Lumen Pay from 2022 to 2024 and now leads product at another company. Sneha Pillai worked alongside me on the same team. Karan Shah was my client for two years. My current manager does not know I am looking, so I have not included her. I am happy for you to speak to her once we have an offer in place.

## Ask before they leave

The easiest time to ask is the week one of you moves on. The work is fresh and nobody is hard to find yet. If your manager has just announced they are leaving, ask now.
